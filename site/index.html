How is this worked out? (plain English)

US crude: “what usually happens this week of the year.” We take the same week (plus the week either side) in each of the last 10 years and average the change. Refineries, driving season and winter heating repeat every year, so this is the most reliable free clue. Example: around early November, the last 10 years averaged a build of about 2.4 million barrels, so that is the forecast.

Cushing: “it keeps going the way it has been going.” Cushing moves in slow waves because pipelines fill and drain it over weeks. So we take roughly 60% of its average change over the last 4 weeks and add a small seasonal nudge. Example: if Cushing rose about 0.6 million a week lately, the forecast is about +0.4 million. The exact weights are re-learned from history every year.

The “likely” range. We check how wrong the forecast was each week over the last 3 years and drop the biggest 10% of misses on each side. The rest sets the range, so roughly 8 weeks in 10 should land inside it.

How we know it works. We replayed every week from 2010 to today, using only data that existed at the time, then compared the forecast with what EIA really reported. We also tried fancier models using production, imports, exports and refinery data; for US crude they were no better, so we kept the simple one.

What it can't do. It can't see tankers, refinery outages or hurricanes. A typical US miss is about 3.5 million barrels, almost as big as a typical week's move, so treat it as “normal for this time of year”, not a prediction to trade on.

Ranges are where 8 in 10 weeks landed over the last 3 years. Most weekly moves are noise (tanker timing, exports), so a forecast here is a baseline to compare the real report against, not a call.

What these images do — and don't — tell us

The imagery can show the facilities and broad surface changes. It cannot resolve tank fill-height differences with sufficient accuracy to compute weekly barrels. Free NAIP aerial imagery is sharper but typically years apart. This dashboard deliberately reports no unverified satellite storage estimates.

TRADE INTERPRETATION

How the signal is formed

When both national crude and Cushing draw, inventories suggest tighter physical supply. When both build, looser supply. Opposite directions produce Mixed. No chart pattern, EIA consensus or macro surprise is inferred.

FORECAST METHOD

Tested before it was trusted

US crude: the average change for the same week (±1 week) over the previous 10 years. Ridge regression and gradient boosting on production, imports, exports, refinery runs and stock gaps did not beat it reliably, so the simple rule stays.

Cushing: a small ridge regression on the 5- and 10-year seasonal averages, how far stocks sit from their 5-year norm, and the last 4 weeks' average change. It beat every simple rule in 2010–17 and in 2018–26.

All accuracy figures come from a walk-forward test: refit each year, scored only on weeks the model had never seen. They are recomputed on every refresh.
